2. What might be simpler as one agent with tools?

For a small shop like Campus Customs, Inventory, Facilities, and Accounting could potentially be combined into one Operations agent with access to the same MCP tools. These jobs overlap when a stock or lease question depends on a bill or checking balance. Ticket 102 shows the clearest case: Facilities and Accounting both checked payment capacity, so a single Operations agent could have gathered the lease facts and cash assessment once.

There is a tradeoff. Ticket 101 required a size-specific stock check and a separate invoice assessment, while Ticket 103 required stock, vendor constraints, and margin analysis. Keeping those specialties distinct can make each review clearer and easier to audit. A practical compromise would be one Operations agent for routine inventory, rent, and cash checks, while preserving separate specialist tools and clear report sections for each subject. Human approval would remain an independent step regardless of agent layout.

Customer Service could remain separate so customer drafts stay focused and do not expose internal cost or cash details. Boss could draft simple messages too, but Boss already triages tickets and reconciles specialist reports; keeping a separate drafting role gives the final reviewer a clear handoff. In a very small team, Customer Service could be combined with Boss if workload stayed low and every draft remained unsent for human review.

3. Three new problems the current app can help handle

Scope: “Handle” here means the agents can inspect recorded facts, prepare a recommendation or approval request, and make a supported database update after a human approves it. The app still does not contact customers, vendors, or landlords, and it does not place or receive physical orders.

  1. An event organizer asks whether a merchandise request can arrive before a specific event date. Boss and Inventory can compare the requested SKU and size with current stock, use desk.date_today and the vendor’s recorded lead time, and flag any open unpaid invoice that blocks shipping. Ticket 101 shows why those checks matter: size-S tees were out of stock and the linked vendor invoice was overdue when the agents ran; paying that invoice later did not confirm shipment. Ticket 103 also shows that a stock shortfall does not identify a confirmed supplier. This new deadline check can identify timing risks and prepare an unsent clarification, but it cannot promise delivery because the database has no SKU-to-vendor link, supplier quote, or guaranteed ship date.
  2. The shop owner wants a cash-impact forecast before approving several recorded bills. Accounting can compare linked invoice and lease amounts and due dates with checking, then explain the combined effect without making a payment. The Cash tab provides a concrete example from the run: the $840.00 invoice was overdue on the shop date, the $2,400.00 rent was due shortly afterward, and paying both from the $3,400.00 starting balance left $160.00. This adds a cross-ticket cash-planning question to the individual reviews in Tickets 101 and 102. Each outflow still requires its own human approval, and the forecast assumes no incoming revenue.
  3. A group asks which sizes of one catalog item are available so it can revise its order mix. Inventory can use get_inventory_for_sku to report the recorded size run instead of considering only one requested size, and Customer Service can draft a factual, unsent reply. For example, the current hoodie records show 4 size S, 8 size M, 14 size L, and 6 size XL; Ticket 103 examined only the requested 20 size-M hoodies and found a 12-unit shortfall. A size-by-size report could help the group choose a different mix, but a human must confirm any substitution or revised quantities. The app cannot reserve stock, change inventory, or promise an order.

These examples stay within the app’s evidence and actions: it can check recorded facts, calculate timing or cash implications, and prepare drafts or approval requests. Missing supplier-to-SKU matches, quotes, confirmed delivery dates, and human decisions remain explicit limits.

4. Three new problems the current app cannot solve yet

  1. A customer returns the wrong item and requests an exchange with a price difference. The app has no order-history, return, exchange, or customer-sale records. If the replacement costs more, the shop must collect money coming in; if it costs less, a refund sends money out. The payment tools currently support invoice, rent, and approved purchase-order payments, but no customer charge or refund. Ticket 101 demonstrates the boundary: it can assess and pay the supplier invoice, but cannot complete the customer’s order. New tools would need order/return records, approved refunds and incoming receipts, and inventory handling for returned goods.
  2. A request to sublease shop space, or a dispute about extra rent arrears. A student vendor might ask to rent part of the store to sell products, or the landlord might claim the shop owes more than the listed monthly rent. The current leases table represents the store’s own lease and next due date; it has no available-space inventory, sublease permission/terms, rent-billing history, or arrears ledger. Ticket 102 shows the supported case—paying the recorded $2,400 monthly rent—not whether the shop may sublet or owes an unrecorded extra amount. Facilities would need sublease/occupancy records and/or rent invoices and an adjustment ledger; Accounting would need a verified amount before preparing any human-approved payment.
  3. An investor asks for historical revenue and profit. The database records cash accounts and outgoing payments, and pricing stores unit costs and list prices, but it has no sales/revenue history, cost-of-goods-sold ledger, payroll, or complete operating-expense history. The Cash tab’s $160.00 is a checking balance after two outflows, not revenue or profit. Ticket 103’s margin calculation is only a per-unit calculation from stored cost and list price; it cannot support an investor’s company-wide financial report. This needs sales and expense records over time, a reporting tool, and an Accounting/reporting workflow.

The three ticket runs show the team is useful for grounded analysis, drafts, and human-approved database actions. The Cash tab and unresolved tickets also show the boundary: a paid bill is not a fulfilled order, a PO draft is not a shipment, and the agents cannot fill gaps that the shop database does not record.
